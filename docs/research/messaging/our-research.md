# Build Connect's messaging yourself, start strict

**Recommendation in one line: build messaging in-house on Connect's existing Postgres database, open a conversation only when a customer sends a call request, keep it text-only, and launch with a short list of legal must-haves.** Renting a chat service would cost between $0 and about $500 a month and would copy tools Connect already has (accounts, blocking, reporting, admin review). A home-built version, by contrast, is small: about four database tables, two screens and one background email job, and it reuses the "check for updates every 2 seconds" pattern the app already has. The product rule that best protects hosts' time is the one used by the paid-call site Clarity, where the customer writes first and the host can only reply ([Clarity Help](https://clarity.fm/help/articles/22/how-do-i-send-a-message-via-the-inbox)). The safety work that cannot wait is mostly paperwork and simple features. Connect must register with the US child-safety hotline (NCMEC) and keep any reported material for a year. It also needs a 48-hour takedown process for intimate images, a report button and two-way blocking inside each conversation, and a plain statement that staff can read reported messages. The biggest open question is legal, not technical: whether to let customers in the UK and EU use messaging at launch, because UK law gives small services no exemption ([Bristows, Jan 2026](https://www.bristows.com/app/uploads/2026/01/The-Safety-Net-Intro1500441324.1.pdf)). The founder decisions are listed at the end.

## Four tables in your own database beat a $279-a-month chat service

The rented chat services split into two kinds. The "complete" products (Stream, Sendbird, TalkJS, CometChat) come with ready-made chat screens, stored history, unread counts and moderation dashboards. The "pipe" services (Pusher, Supabase Realtime, Ably, LiveKit) only deliver messages instantly, so Connect would still have to build storage, unread counts and email alerts itself. Their prices at Connect's expected size look like this:

| Service | Live at 100 users | At 10,000 monthly users | Catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stream | $0 (free up to 1,000 users; 2,000 on its startup "Maker" plan) | $399–$499/mo | Jumps from $0 straight to $399; only 100 people online at once on free plans ([Stream pricing](https://getstream.io/chat/pricing/); [Stream Maker](https://getstream.io/maker-account/)) |
| TalkJS | $279/mo | $279/mo | No free live plan; flat price up to 10,000 users ([TalkJS pricing](https://talkjs.com/pricing/)) |
| Sendbird | $0 (developer plan, 20 people online at once) | about $499/mo, from a price table labelled 2021 | Deletes messages after 6 months on cheaper plans ([Sendbird pricing](https://sendbird.com/pricing/chat)) |
| CometChat | from $239/mo | not published | Free only for testing ([CometChat pricing](https://www.cometchat.com/pricing)) |
| LiveKit (already used for video) | $0 | n/a | Not usable: messages reach only people in the call at that moment and are never stored ([LiveKit docs](https://docs.livekit.io/home/client/data/text-streams/)) |

Renting has three real drawbacks for Connect. First, it duplicates what exists: Connect's own block list would have to be copied into the vendor's system on every block and unblock, or checked through a "pre-send hook" (a step where the vendor asks Connect's server before delivering each message). Only Stream offers that hook on every plan ([Stream pricing](https://getstream.io/chat/pricing/)). Second, messages would live on someone else's servers, and some vendors delete them after six months unless you pay more ([Sendbird pricing](https://sendbird.com/pricing/chat); [CometChat pricing](https://www.cometchat.com/pricing)). Third, vendors change their plans often. Sendbird dropped its free plan in early 2024 and later brought a version back ([PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/sendbird/diffs/2024Q1)). Ably has changed its pricing model twice in two years ([Ably blog](https://ably.com/blog/new-ably-per-minute-consumption-pricing); [AgentDeals digest](https://agentdeals.dev/digest/2026-w45)). Stream says its free startup plan has "limited" availability ([Stream Maker](https://getstream.io/maker-account/)).

The well-known warnings against building chat yourself almost all come from chat vendors. Sendbird claims basic chat costs $300,000–$500,000 in staff time in the first year ([Sendbird](https://sendbird.com/blog/build-vs-buy-build-chat-from-scratch-or-jump-start-with-a-chat-api-and-sdk)), and its technology chief wrote that his own two in-house attempts failed ([Sendbird, 2019](https://sendbird.com/blog/dont-try-this-at-home-why-you-shouldnt-build-chat-in-house)). Those figures assume group chat, phone apps, typing indicators and millions of users. Connect needs none of that. It needs one-to-one, text-only, web-only threads between two known people. The researchers found no independent story from a small team that regretted building something this simple, and that absence is telling.

The build itself is modest. It needs a conversations table and a participants table (which remembers where each person stopped reading, and doubles as the check that a user belongs to a conversation). It also needs a messages table and a reports table. Screens update by polling, which means the browser quietly asks the server "anything new?" every few seconds, as Connect's call screens already do. As a rough estimate, 50 people with a chat open, each polling every 3 seconds, is about 17 tiny requests a second, which one database handles easily. Faster "push" methods exist, such as server-sent events (a connection the server keeps open to send updates). On Vercel, however, those connections are cut off after 300 to 800 seconds, and each open one keeps a paid server running ([Vercel limits](https://vercel.com/docs/functions/limitations)). That makes polling the cheaper choice at this size. If Connect ever needs to rent, Stream is the pick, because its free plan covers the first 1,000 to 2,000 users and its pre-send hook can respect Connect's own block list. Keeping a copy of every message in Connect's own database from day one keeps that door open.

## Customers write first, hosts only reply, and a call request opens the thread

Marketplaces use four basic patterns. In the first, a free first message lets the buyer start and the seller reply (Clarity, MentorCruise, Airbnb). In the second, nobody can message until a booking exists (TaskRabbit, PAAQ). In the third, every message costs money (Cameo, Topmate). In the fourth, only paying members can message (Patreon, Passes). Paid-call sites cluster around one rule. Clarity states it in a single sentence: experts "can only reply" if the member wrote first or sent a call request, and a call request turns messaging on automatically ([Clarity Help](https://clarity.fm/help/articles/22/how-do-i-send-a-message-via-the-inbox)). MentorCruise works the same way. A first message becomes a "message request", the mentor's reply opens the chat, and mentors can close a request at any time with no penalty ([MentorCruise Help](https://help.mentorcruise.com/article/68-how-can-i-respond-to-inquiries)). TaskRabbit is stricter and allows no contact before booking, citing privacy ([TaskRabbit Support](https://support.taskrabbit.com/hc/en-us/articles/360035010172-Can-I-Contact-a-Tasker-Before-Booking-a-Task)).

Flooding is a real risk for Connect's hosts, who look a lot like public figures. A 2022 study counted 8,717 direct messages sent to five well-known women in one month. About 1 in 15 broke Instagram's rules, including 125 cases of image-based sexual abuse ([CCDH](https://counterhate.com/blog/dm-abuse-instagram-negligent-over-misogynist-harassment-of-women-in-the-public-eye-study-finds/)). Creator platforms answer this with gates: price the message (Topmate, Cameo), allow messages only from paying members (Patreon), or allow replies only to people the creator wrote to first (Ko-fi) ([Topmate Help](https://topmate72420835211887570.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/1070000111288-what-s-a-priority-dm-); [Patreon Help](https://support.patreon.com/hc/es-es/articles/203913429); [Ko-fi Help](https://help.ko-fi.com/hc/en-us/articles/360016956178-Direct-Messages-on-Ko-fi)).

For Connect, the recommended rules are these. A conversation opens only when a customer sends a call request, and the request includes a short note about what they want to discuss, as PAAQ does ([PAAQ Help](https://intercom.help/paaq/en/articles/11452858-can-i-chat-with-someone-before-booking)). Hosts cannot start conversations with customers. If the host declines or blocks, the thread closes. After a call, the thread stays open for a short follow-up window and then becomes read-only until the next booking. That covers what call marketplaces actually use messaging for: agreeing the topic, sending background beforehand, sorting out timing, and a short thank-you afterwards ([Clarity Help](https://clarity.fm/help/articles/35/how-can-experts-best-prepare-for-a-call)). It also gives hosts no unpaid inbox to manage. Free "ask a question before booking" messages and paid messages are best added later as switches each host can turn on. Starting strict and loosening is easier on users than the reverse. Bumble's loosening of its "women message first" rule was welcomed ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2232004/bumble-lets-either-person-send-first-message)), while Thumbtack's restrictive phone-number masking drew a long backlash from its professionals ([Thumbtack community](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/comment/7597)). This lesson is an inference, not a measured study, and nobody has published data on whether pre-booking chat raises bookings.

To keep payment on the platform, copy the approach of the big marketplaces. Airbnb's rule is that all contact before booking stays on Airbnb, and it screens for "words or numbers that might include contact information" ([Airbnb Help](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/209)). Fiverr and Upwork ban sharing contact details to do business elsewhere, with penalties up to closing the account ([Fiverr](https://help.fiverr.com/hc/en-us/articles/38728907371665); [Upwork community](https://community.upwork.com/t5/Freelancers/Important-Reminder-Policy-on-Sharing-Contact-Information/m-p/1586109)). No platform has published how well this filtering works. Hard blocking also annoys honest users ([Thumbtack community](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/comment/7588/)). For a small product, a gentle warning works better: when a message contains an email address, phone number or payment-app name (Venmo, Cash App, a crypto wallet), show the sender a reminder of the rule and quietly flag the message for admin review. Marketplace operators also point out that the strongest protection is value, meaning secure payment, scheduling and reviews that make staying worthwhile ([Sharetribe](https://www.sharetribe.com/academy/how-to-discourage-people-from-going-around-your-payment-system/)).

## Seven safety must-haves on day one, mostly paperwork

The moment Connect stores private messages, US law treats it as an "electronic communication service". That brings duties even for text-only chat. Connect must report child sexual abuse material, child sex trafficking and online enticement of a minor to NCMEC's CyberTipline (the national reporting hotline) when it actually learns of them. Since the 2024 REPORT Act, it must keep reported material for one year rather than 90 days ([WSGR](https://wsgr.com/en/insights/new-minor-safety-obligations-for-online-services-report-act-expands-child-sexual-exploitation-reporting-requirements.html); [Public Law 118-59](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ59/html/PLAW-118publ59.htm)). Enticement is a text-based crime, so the duty applies fully to text messaging. The scale is real: NCMEC received about 1.4 million enticement reports in 2025, and more than 50,000 financial sextortion reports ([NCMEC data](https://ncmec.org/cybertiplinedata); [NCMEC 2026](https://us.missingkids.org/blog/2026/ncmec-releases-new-sextortion-data-2025)). The law does not require Connect to scan messages proactively ([18 U.S.C. 2258A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A)). Separately, the Take It Down Act has applied to platforms since 19 May 2026. It requires a clear way for anyone to ask for removal of intimate images shared without consent, removal within 48 hours, and fines of up to $53,088 per violation ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act); [Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/united-states/federal-trade-commission/the-take-it-down-acts-notice-and-removal-requirements-enter-into-effect/); [GRC Report](https://www.grcreport.com/post/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act-as-platforms-face-48-hour-removal-mandate)). Whether it reaches private one-to-one messages is unsettled, but Connect as a whole very likely counts as a covered platform.

A US law called the Stored Communications Act bars a messaging provider from revealing message contents, with exceptions that include user consent and reports to NCMEC ([18 U.S.C. 2702](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2702)). Platforms rely on their terms and privacy policy as that consent. So Connect's policy must say plainly that messages are stored by Connect, are not end-to-end encrypted (a design where even the company cannot read them), may be read by staff when reported or flagged, and may be shared with NCMEC or police as the law allows. End-to-end encryption would make reviewing reports impossible, so it should not be built.

Pulling this together, the day-one list is: (1) register with NCMEC and write a one-page procedure for what an admin does when a report suggests a minor or exploitation; (2) a report button inside each conversation that saves a locked copy of the recent messages, kept for at least a year; (3) blocking that stops messages in both directions, wired to Connect's existing block tool; (4) a Take It Down removal form with a 48-hour target; (5) privacy and terms wording as above; (6) admin access to conversations only when reported or flagged, with every view recorded in a log; and (7) a law-enforcement contact email. Under the federal children's privacy law (COPPA), Connect also needs a written step for closing an account fast if it learns a user is under 13 ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/index%2Ephp/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect)). The booking-gated rule from the previous section is itself a major safety measure. Twitch's main fix after predator reports was to stop strangers from messaging users and to require phone verification to send private messages ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/twitch-strengthens-methods-to-combat-child-predators)). Connect's riskiest scenario is a minor posing as an adult customer, so a host messaging only people who have requested a paid call removes most cold contact.

UK and EU customers raise the hardest question. The UK Online Safety Act has no small-service exemption. Any user-to-user service with UK users must complete a written illegal-content risk assessment and apply the measures in Ofcom's codes ([Bristows](https://www.bristows.com/app/uploads/2026/01/The-Safety-Net-Intro1500441324.1.pdf); [Ofcom](https://ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/information-for-industry/guide-for-services/risk-assessments)). Ofcom treats direct messaging as a risk feature ([Ofcom codes](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/codes-of-practice)). The EU's Digital Services Act spares very small companies most platform duties, but every host of user content still needs a notice-and-action channel, a way to receive and act on complaints ([DSA Art. 19](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dsa/dsa-regulation/article-19/)). EU privacy rules also restrict automatic scanning of private messages ([eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/interim-rules-on-voluntary-csam-detection-reinstated-until-2028/)). The cheapest path is to switch messaging off for UK and EU customers at launch. They could still book calls, and their booking note would carry their question. That choice belongs to the founder and a lawyer.

Several things can wait. Paid AI classifiers can wait: Thorn's tools are custom-quoted, and Thorn deals have run $50,000–$80,000 a year ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/thorn)). Image scanning can wait until images are allowed, and Cloudflare's tool is free then ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/a-simpler-path-to-a-safer-internet-an-update-to-our-csam-scanning-tool/)). Transparency reports, formal appeals beyond an email address, moderation in many languages, and full UK and EU compliance programmes can also wait, if those regions are switched off. Both Ofcom and industry frameworks scale duties to a service's size and risk ([Ofcom](https://ofcom.org.uk/news-centre/2024/why-size-and-risk-matter-in-our-approach-to-online-safety)).

## The first version: text threads, unread dots and one email job

The smallest version that is both useful and safe has two screens: an inbox listing conversations with an unread dot, and a thread view with a text box. Messages are plain text up to about 2,000 characters. Links appear as plain text rather than clickable links, which blocks a known trick where a disguised "javascript:" link runs harmful code ([OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-01-15-prevent-xss-attacks-react/view)). A small "Seen" label is nearly free in one-to-one chat, because it uses the same "last read" marker as the unread dot. Each customer gets a limit on how many new call-request threads they can open per day, with tighter limits for brand-new accounts, much like Fiverr's 60-minute pause after too many messages to strangers ([Fiverr Help](https://help.fiverr.com/hc/en-us/articles/37030193818257)). Deleting a message hides it from view but keeps a copy for safety reviews. Each send carries a one-time code from the browser, so a flaky connection cannot post the same message twice ([OpenReplay](https://blog.openreplay.com/idempotency-api/)). Every server action must check that the user belongs to the conversation, because skipping that check is the classic way someone reads another person's messages by changing a number in the web address.

Email, Connect's only notification channel, fits the existing background helper. Every few minutes the helper looks for people who have unread messages older than about 10–15 minutes and have not yet been emailed about them. It sends one email per person ("You have a new message from Sam"), with a link and without the message text. That gives at most one email per batch of messages, skips anyone who is mid-conversation, and keeps private content out of inboxes.

Some features should explicitly wait. Typing indicators and "online now" dots need second-by-second updates that polling cannot provide. Sending photos and files brings image-scanning duties. Link previews let the server be tricked into fetching internal addresses. Paid messages add refunds and disputes, which Cameo handles by making messages non-refundable even when the talent never replies ([Cameo Help](https://help.cameo.com/en/support/solutions/articles/43000661378-what-s-a-cameo-direct-message-)). Message editing, search, phone push notifications, live push connections, and free pre-booking questions can also wait. Signs that it is time to revisit the in-house choice are native phone apps, group chats, attachments, or thousands of people chatting at the same moment.

## Decisions only the founder can make

The recommendation stands on its own. Build in-house on Postgres with polling and an email digest, and allow threads only from a call request, with hosts unable to start them. Keep it text-only with soft contact-detail warnings, and ship the seven safety must-haves. Rent nothing. That adds no new monthly bill and no new vendor. Some choices, though, are judgement calls that only the founder (with a lawyer where marked) should make:

| Decision | Recommended default | Why it is yours |
|---|---|---|
| Can customers send a free question before requesting a call? | No at launch; add later as a per-host switch | Trades host protection against possible extra bookings; no data shows which wins |
| How long does a thread stay open after a call? | 7 days, then read-only until the next booking | Sets how much unpaid follow-up hosts give |
| Messaging for UK and EU customers at launch? | Off, until a lawyer scopes the UK risk assessment (lawyer decides) | Legal exposure versus a worldwide audience |
| Contact details in messages: warn or block? | Warn the sender and flag for review | Strictness versus annoying honest users |
| How long to keep messages after an account closes? | Delete 30–90 days after closure; keep reported conversations at least 1 year (lawyer confirms) | Privacy promise versus evidence needs |
| Privacy and terms wording on staff review and NCMEC reporting | Plain disclosure as described above (lawyer drafts) | It is your legal consent basis |
| Paid messages as a future product? | Not in the first version | New revenue versus refund and dispute work |

## Conclusion

The core finding reverses the usual advice: for Connect, the hard part of messaging is not the technology but the rules and the legal duties. Those stay the same whether the chat is rented or built. A vendor would not register Connect with NCMEC, write its takedown process, decide what hosts owe customers between calls, or settle whether UK users are in scope. Renting therefore buys polish Connect does not need, while leaving every hard question in place.

The deeper product insight is that, for hosts who sell their time, messaging should be part of the booking rather than a feature beside it. Tying every conversation to a paid call request protects hosts' time, keeps payment on the platform and removes most stranger-to-stranger risk, all with one rule. Every looser option (free questions, paid messages, host-started outreach) can be switched on later once real usage shows it is wanted.
